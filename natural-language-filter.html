Natural Language Filter

At Intapp, I led the design of Natural Language Filter for DealCloud, a CRM used by thousands of financial services professionals across private equity, investment banking, and M&A advisory. You type a question in plain English, and the system turns it into filters you can see, check, and change.

Role
Lead designer
Focus
Query & data structure
Discipline
Web design
Year
2024
The problem

DealCloud is highly customizable, but that flexibility made filters feel foreign to anyone who hadn't set them up. To answer "Which Tier A clients haven't been contacted in the last four months?" you had to know your firm's field names, dig through menus, and stack conditions in the right order.

The relationship managers and partners who needed answers most had quietly stopped using filters. When I sat with them, I didn't hear frustration. I heard resignation.

DealCloud had all the data. The system spoke schema and users spoke intent.

And because these users act on what they see, a black-box answer wouldn't do. They needed to be able to check it.

The question

How might we let people ask in their own words, and still show them exactly what the system understood?

What I noticed
  • People don't start in advanced search. The PM's first ask was to put this in advanced search. But when I watched people work, they didn't go there to find information. They went straight to the object pages, like Companies. So I proposed building it there, where the question already starts.
  • The problem was translation, not data. Users knew what they wanted, just not what the system called it.
  • Filters were already the right output. Instead of inventing a new kind of answer, the AI could build the filters people already knew, so everything downstream kept working.
  • A wrong reading you can see is still useful. Mostly right and fully visible beats correct and hidden.
Exploration
  • Conversational overlay: easy to ask, but you couldn't tweak one condition without starting over.
  • AI-enhanced filter builder: familiar, but suggestions don't help if you don't know what fields exist.
  • Integrated view builder (chosen): plain language in, editable filter tokens out, right in context. Ask like a conversation, adjust like a filter.

I pushed for the third. For regulated professionals who need to trust what they see before acting on it, that transparency wasn't optional.

The hard part

When the AI reads you wrong. Every firm names things differently, so misreads will happen. I designed them to be cheap to catch and fix:

  • Prompt guidance shows which kinds of questions work.
  • The loading state lists each condition as it's set, so you watch the translation happen.
  • Each condition is its own token, so you can fix one without redoing the rest.
  • One step takes you back to your previous view.
Final solution

Generated filters use DealCloud's existing filter model, so people inspect and adjust them with controls they already know.

Prompt guidance

Examples show what you can ask, without having to learn the data model.

Loading state

Instead of a spinner, you see each filter being set.

Editable filters

The output is tokens, not a black box. You can adjust any condition without starting over.

Workflow continuity

Return to your previous grid view in one step, switch between AI and manual filters, and save an AI-made view for the team to reuse.

Tradeoffs
  • Filters, not free-text answers. Easier to check, but limited to what filters can express.
  • Chat is where you ask; tokens are where you're in control. We didn't make chat the main interface.
  • The standard filter stays. People move freely between the two.
My role

I was the lead designer. I owned the interaction model, the guidance and loading states, the token output, and the connection to saved views. I made the case for two key calls: building it into the object pages instead of advanced search, and the integrated view builder direction.

Impact

Formal adoption metrics weren't available at the time. Early feedback still showed people using it as a starting point and refining from there.

10 of 10 internal testers said they would use it over the standard filter.

Reflection

The AI was most useful as a translator, not an answer machine. Showing the interpretation made mistakes visible, and visible mistakes are what let people trust the rest.
